# Research paper review: incremental game balance and motivation

**Scope and evidence standard (30 September 2026).** This review reads primary academic work on idle games and adjacent research on goals, waiting, and game motivation. A paper's observation of another game or population is **not** proof that a particular Rocket Raja balance change will improve retention. Below, **finding** means the authors observed or reported it; **design inference** means a testable application to Rocket Raja. Sources are linked to the publisher, institutional copy, or author manuscript, with a DOI where available. The [Rocket Raja economy audit](Rocket%20Raja%20Economy%20Audit.md) supplies the project-specific diagnosis; it is separate from the external evidence here.

## What the evidence implies for the current design

### 1. Early products lose both economic value and decision value

**Project observation.** In the [economy audit](Rocket%20Raja%20Economy%20Audit.md), the eight products have approximately 8× jumps in both base cost and base income, while level bonuses chiefly reward owning more of an individual product. In the audited all-products-at-level-1 state, the newest family supplies about **87.5%** of passive income and the first supplies roughly **0.0000417%**. This is a mathematical project finding, not a result from an external paper.

**Research connection.** The taxonomy distinguishes multiple resource/generator arrangements and late-game planning, while the *Kittens* close reading shows how old mechanics can remain part of new planning problems. Ryan et al. explain why visible competence and agency matter to enjoyment. None of those papers says old generators must retain equal income share. **Design inference:** give early products a recurring, legible role—such as cross-product milestones, a portfolio bonus, a time-limited production specialty, or a useful upgrade choice—then determine whether players actually consider them again. A passive percentage alone is an incomplete relevance test; ask whether spending on an old product ever beats or complements the next obvious new-product purchase.

**Measure before and after:** each product's income share, the marginal income per rupee of its next purchase, time between rational purchases of each family, which products players voluntarily revisit, and whether a portfolio milestone creates an interesting timing decision or merely another automatic purchase. Reject a proposed synergy if it makes every optimal run follow one fixed upgrade order.

### 2. Tapping can remain satisfying without becoming compulsory labor

**Project observation.** Tap income scales on a distinct track from product output. An instrumented current-catalog run shows the instantaneous rate of **three manual taps per second** falling from **35.94% of normal product income at 5:00** to **0.00225% when all products are owned at 7:31**, then **0.000002051% at first prestige**. These are automatic-policy checkpoints, not a population estimate or cumulative tap share; see the [comprehensive audit](Comprehensive%20Design%20Audit.md#fresh-progression-trace-and-method). The design problem is that the tap payoff can become imperceptible as production climbs.

**Research connection.** Alharthi et al. observe that early clicking often cedes importance to automation, so a falling tap share is not inherently a defect. Their examples preserve optional moments of active skill or short-term acceleration. Hwang's small study reports that tapping felt rewarding when it visibly sped progress. Tepponen et al. show why an action done only to fill an otherwise empty wait can feel hollow. **Design inference:** make tapping a meaningful opt-in accelerator with a noticeable and explained payoff—possibly through a bounded link to current business output or a short, strategic burst—while checking that the best path does not demand continuous fast tapping. A permanent target like “taps must provide X% of income” is **not supplied by these papers**; establish it by playtest.

**Measure:** actual taps per active minute, incremental purchase time saved by tapping, active versus passive progress, players' perceived effort/reward, and whether low-tap players can still reach core milestones in an acceptable time. Test across early, mid, and post-prestige runs, not just a late synthetic state.

### 3. Prestige needs a clear reason to reset and a different next run

**Project observation.** Current prestige first requires every product family and a high run-income threshold; the audit reports a **46:26** first-prestige time under its specified deterministic active policy, then **12:36** and **9:10** on later runs. It grants five reputation at the threshold, spent in a fixed tree of 12 five-rank Legacy perks, with no other spending route in the audited design. These are simulator results under one policy, not measured player times.

**Research connection.** The *Kittens* close reading describes three useful pieces: a reset preview, a persistent benefit that changes the next run, and a timing choice. The goal-gradient studies warn of a motivation trough immediately after a reward; they suggest investigating the moment after a prestige. Ryan et al. provide an explanation for why a reset with a distinct choice and visible new capability may feel better than a reset whose only obvious result is “numbers rise faster.” **Design inference:** show the reputation payout and estimated recovery before confirming reset; put one new short-term target directly after reset; make at least some Legacy spending represent competing strategies rather than an invariant rank order. This is a hypothesis for player testing, not proof that a branching tree automatically improves retention.

**Measure:** percentage of players who understand the reset payoff, time from reset to the previous income level, time to the first new decision, second-prestige return rate, diversity of Legacy choices, and whether any option becomes objectively dominant. Measure “reset regret” with a direct short survey; the cited work does not provide a numeric threshold.

### 4. Waiting should show purpose, choice, and an arrival point

**Research connection.** Waiting research does not condemn delay itself: a delay can create planning and anticipation if its purpose and result are clear. The idle-game taxonomy recommends interface support such as queue forecasts and time-to-completion cues. Hwang's participants valued away progress but could be disappointed by the amount waiting produced. Goal-gradient work suggests a distant, invisible goal can elicit a different pattern of effort from a visible near goal. **Design inference:** show time to the next affordable purchase and to the next meaningful milestone under the player's current rate, show what offline time actually earned, and give a few optional strategic decisions during long waits. A deliberately paced prestige can be good; a long span with no understandable intermediate change is a risk to test.

**Measure:** longest interval with no useful purchase or unlock, forecast error, proportion of returns with an affordable decision, player understanding of the next goal, and wait-related abandonment points. Evaluate active and offline policies separately. Do not infer that maximizing purchases per minute is the goal.

### 5. Balance simulations need player-centered outcomes

The economy-optimization paper supports repeated simulation under different policies, but its simulated optimum is only as good as its objective. The *Neko Atsume* survey shows that check-in frequency and time spent playing capture different engagement dimensions. Thus a single “time to first prestige” score cannot stand in for game health. A useful Rocket Raja evaluation should compare at least active tapping, low-tap/automation, occasional return, and offline-focused policies, then combine simulation traces with observed player comprehension and preference. Record acquisition times, dead intervals, product use, tap contribution, and prestige recovery **alongside** return behavior and perceived agency. This measurement program is a design inference assembled from the sources, not a validated universal battery.

## Priority research hypotheses for playtests

| Hypothesis to test | Intervention to compare with current build | Evidence that would disconfirm it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Old products feel relevant when they create choices across the portfolio. | Milestone or cross-product bonus with an explicit preview of its next payoff. | Players still ignore early products, or everyone follows one trivial purchase script. |
| Active play feels valuable when the incremental reward is visible and bounded. | Clear tap-to-next-purchase estimate and optional burst/production-linked tap variant. | No improvement in perceived agency, or low-tap players feel punished. |
| A legible reset gives prestige a stronger purpose. | Preview payout and recovery, plus a small number of genuinely competing permanent directions. | Players cannot explain their choice, regret resetting, or one direction dominates. |
| Clear waiting goals improve the experience of slow stretches. | Next-purchase/milestone ETA and accurate offline earnings explanation. | Forecasts are inaccurate, frustration persists, or the extra UI adds confusion. |
| Improving the first reset alone will not guarantee a healthier game. | Track at least three cycles and multiple engagement measures. | First reset becomes faster but second-run choices or return rates worsen. |

## What these papers do **not** answer

No reviewed academic paper establishes a universal optimal product-income distribution, minimum profitable tap share, first-prestige time, reputation payout curve, or retention effect for Rocket Raja. Most direct idle-game studies are observational, qualitative, or short; the strongest goal/motivation experiments occur outside idle games. The research therefore narrows the design questions and identifies failure modes, while [Balance Experiment Plan](Balance%20Experiment%20Plan.md) and actual player tests must set the numeric targets. In particular, do not present a simulated 46-minute first prestige or a favorable spreadsheet curve as proof of a fun first session.
